Privacy Tests Can Make AI Look Better at Keeping Secrets

How seemingly innocuous naming choices can inflate measured privacy protection in language models.

Mayank SaxenaOctober 2026Code and data

In short

    01The main finding

    Benchmark authors often replace people's names with labels such as “Person A” and assume the change is neutral. In two privacy benchmarks it was not neutral. With placeholders, four language models kept private details out of their replies more often on ConfAIde's short stories. On PrivacyLens agent tasks the estimate is inconclusive. Swapping in other realistic names did not raise protection. A privacy score can depend on how a benchmark writes its people, not only on the model.

    02The experiment

    ConfAIde (Mireshghallah et al. 2023) presents short stories about a character asked to reveal a friend's secret. PrivacyLens (Shao et al. 2024) presents agent tasks, where a model uses tools such as email or notes to write a message or post while records contain private details the task does not need. Each benchmark contributed 120 fictional scenarios.

    The four main open models were Qwen2.5-7B (Qwen Team 2024), Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al. 2024), Mistral-7B (Jiang et al. 2023) and Gemma-2-9B (Gemma Team 2024). For each scenario, I compared the published wording, placeholder names and a control using other realistic names. Protection is the share of replies Claude Sonnet judged to keep the private detail out; its agreement with human labels is documented in Appendix B. See the appendix for reply counts and prompt-version details.

    03What changed the score?

    Figure 1 shows the pre-specified comparison and the split by benchmark. The control is what makes the result interpretable: giving everyone a different realistic name did not raise protection on this one draw, so the increase showed up for placeholder labels. What about placeholders matters (their brevity, their anonymized look, or the loss of a name to give away) is still unknown. Possible explanations, none of them tested here:

    Because story scores started low, the increase relative to the published names was about a third to more than half for three of the four models. This is a relative change in these benchmark scores, not a general correction factor.

    Why the benchmarks differ is unknown. One possibility is that a name is the main handle for disclosure in a short story, while agent tasks also expose private details in records and tool outputs. This study did not test that explanation.

    04What “protected” can mean

    A higher protection score is not always better privacy judgment. A blanket refusal can count as protection even when the model could have completed the harmless part of a task. Asked what one colleague should say to another, Llama replied in full: “” That reply is scored as protected. And a reply can avoid a person's name while still revealing details that identify them. The examples below show one case of genuine withholding and one such limitation. They are illustrative, not evidence for the averages.

    05What evaluators can take from this

    When evaluating privacy behavior, compare realistic names with placeholders on the same items, inspect what “protected” replies actually do, and test more than one task format. Report which naming condition a score uses. The same goes for interpretability work, where names are often swapped to build counterfactual prompts: realistic renaming did not move behavior here, but placeholder-style swaps did.

    The result is limited to the two benchmarks, four small open models and prompts tested. Names were realistically reassigned only once, and there was no length-matched control. In 41 PrivacyLens items, a real name remained inside a username or email handle after placeholder replacement; without them, the PrivacyLens estimate is about zero and realistic renaming lowers protection slightly. The grader can see “Person A” in a reply; a check found no sign of leniency, but a sample this small cannot rule out a bias of a few points. A clear effect showed up on one of the two task formats, so this is not a general correction factor. The binary score does not distinguish genuine withholding from blanket refusal, and the study did not test appropriate sharing. All scenarios used fictional people. This is not evidence that models disclose real people's information in deployment.

    A mechanistic next step. The models are open, so the explanations above can be tested on ConfAIde items where the published version leaks and the placeholder version protects:

    • Measure: cut a leaking reply just before the secret, give both versions that same start, and compare how strongly each favors the secret's first word over a deflection.
    • Locate: patch activations between the two versions, layer by layer and position by position (Heimersheim & Nanda 2024).
    • Attend: compare attention from the reply to the secret owner's name.
    • Vary the label: “Person A/B”, realistic names sharing a surname, bare letters, and distinct short names such as “Zed”/“Quin”, which separate a repeated word from an anonymized look.

    06Other findings

    In secondary tests, a privacy instruction raised protection; test-like formatting and a scoring note had little effect. The benchmark-awareness probe gave no support for a recognition account in the one model whose self-reports varied, though it cannot rule one out.     Instruction placement, benchmark awareness and test cues

    Where the instruction goes

    An explicit privacy instruction was also measured. It was not combined with placeholders and was not tested for its cost to appropriate sharing. The reference instruction is one sentence in the system prompt: "" Another version put the same sentence at the end of the user's message.

    Does benchmark awareness explain the effect?

    One possible explanation for any benchmark effect is that models behave differently when they notice they are being tested. In a separate call, each model saw every prompt version and was asked whether it looked like a test or a real request. This measures what a model says, not whether it has seen these benchmarks in training, and it cannot detect recognition that a model does not report.

    Qwen and Mistral answered "real" to nearly everything and Gemma answered "test" to everything, so only Llama's answers varied enough to compare with its behavior. For Llama, placeholder names did not raise its "test" answers ( points on ConfAIde, not a clear change; on PrivacyLens), while protection rose on ConfAIde. In Llama, then, this probe gives no support for the idea that placeholders work by making prompts look like tests; with the other three models at floor or ceiling, it cannot rule that out.

    Test cues and other negative results

    Looking like a test did not matter. The study was first designed to test whether test-like cues raise protection. They mostly did not. A test-case wrapper (### Case / ### Input / ### Response) changed protection by points (), and a note saying the reply would be scored by (), statistically equivalent to zero. All three cues together gave , about what the placeholder part of the bundle alone predicts (interaction , ). What I learned: the feature that moved behavior was not the one that makes a prompt look most like a test. What remains uncertain: these are only two kinds of test cue, in small models.

    Methods and checks

    Supporting detail for the numbers above. Open any section.

    Appendix A: estimates and intervals

    Each pre-specified estimate is the item-paired difference in protection between a version and the published prompt (120 items per benchmark), with benchmarks weighted equally, pooled over models with DerSimonian–Laird random effects (DerSimonian & Laird 1986). Intervals come from a cluster bootstrap over items. The four single changes were Holm-corrected together (Holm 1979); ±3 points were the pre-specified equivalence bounds (Lakens 2017). A test passed if its Holm-adjusted p-value was below 0.05 and at least three of four models agreed in sign; the intervals shown are unadjusted 95% intervals. The benchmark split in Figure 1 was not pre-specified: it averages the item-level difference over the four models and bootstraps over items (4,000 resamples). Unclear replies are excluded from every rate.

    Appendix B: can we trust the grades?

    Before any result was read, I graded 150 pilot replies by hand, all from Qwen2.5-7B, blind to model and version; 147 had a clear verdict. One annotator (me) set this check. Claude Sonnet then had to agree with those labels at Cohen's κ (Cohen 1960) ≥ 0.70 before it could grade the study. Long agent traces were shown to Sonnet in a shortened view (the whole user message up to 2,500 characters, otherwise its first and last paragraphs), and the gate was re-run on that view. On the 62 check replies written with placeholders, agreement was just as high (κ = 0.93), and Sonnet never called a reply protected that I had marked as leaked, so there is no sign that it graded placeholder replies leniently; a sample this small cannot rule out a bias of a few points.

    Appendix C: pre-specified plan and deviations

    The design, the 240 test items (never used before in this project, plus 60 pilot items), the decision rules and the analysis code were written down and committed with checksums before generation: a self-registered plan, not an external registry. Eleven dated deviations are documented with it. The largest: the registered scorer was Qwen2.5-32B with a misclassification correction; its agreement with human labels was κ = 0.57, so the reported results use Sonnet, which graded every reply after passing the gate. The plan's correction family had five tests and the analysis used four; with all five, placeholders and the instruction remain significant. The planned activation probe for recognition was not run. Another is that Gemma answered "test" to every recognition prompt, so it could not be used for the recognition comparison.

    Appendix D: generation settings

    vLLM 0.34 (Kwon et al. 2023), temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95, 384 new tokens, 4 samples per prompt, pinned model revisions. Placeholder names replace every person with "Person A", "Person B" and so on in order of appearance, and email addresses with userN@example.com; realistic renaming uses a fixed name list in the same positions. The recognition probe is a separate greedy call per prompt with a one-word answer (test, real or unsure).

    Appendix F: reply counts and study arms

    The main study used four models, 240 items, 12 versions and four samples per version. A pre-specified neuron-steering arm is not reported here because it does not vary naming; its results are in the code repository. A separate recognition probe was not graded.
