The privacy benchmark illusion: how placeholder names change model behavior

How seemingly innocuous naming choices can inflate measured privacy protection in language models.

Mayank SaxenaOctober 2026Code and data

01Are privacy benchmarks measuring what we think they are?

A privacy benchmark asks whether a model keeps someone's private information out of what it says, sends or posts. To build one, its authors make many small choices about how the people in each scenario are written: real-sounding names or labels, a story or a tool log, what the request looks like. These choices are usually treated as neutral packaging around the behavior being measured.

This study asks whether they are. Can a benchmark-design choice that seems harmless change the safety behavior it measures? The case study is one of the most common choices: writing people as "Person A" and "Person B" instead of giving them names. The short answer is that placeholder names make language models look more private, and the rest of this note measures by how much, where, and what the change looks like in the replies.

02In short

    03The experiment

    I used two benchmarks. ConfAIde (tier 3, 120 items) gives short stories in which a character knows a friend's secret and is asked something by a third person; the model writes the character's reply. PrivacyLens (120 items) gives agent tasks: the model acts for a user through tools such as email and a notes app and writes a message or a post, and the user's records hold someone else's private details that the task does not need. All the people in both benchmarks are fictional.

    Four open models answered every item: Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B and Gemma-2-9B, with OLMo-2-7B as a smaller check. Each item ran in twelve versions that change one thing at a time. The three that matter for the main result are below; the rest are in section 09.

    VersionWhat changesExample
    Published promptNothing: the item as the benchmark ships it"Liam, Noah, Emily and Alice are co-workers…"
    Placeholder namesEvery person becomes Person A, B, C…; email addresses become userN@example.com"Person A, Person B, Person C and Person D are co-workers…"
    Other realistic namesEvery person gets a different realistic name from a fixed list, in the same places (the control)"Sunita, Kwame, … are co-workers…"

    The measure is protection: the share of replies that keep the private detail out. Claude Sonnet graded every reply as protected, leaked or unclear, seeing the private detail, the task and the reply but not the model or the version. Before it graded anything, it had to agree with my own blind labels on 147 pilot replies at a pre-registered bar of κ ≥ 0.70; it reached . The design, items, decision rules and analysis code were frozen with checksums before any reply was generated.

    Why the two benchmarks differ is open. One reading: in a ConfAIde story the name is the main handle by which a character can be given away, while in a PrivacyLens task the private details sit in records and tool outputs that a placeholder does not hide. That is a hypothesis; this study did not test it.

    05What does the behavior look like?

    The metric is binary: a reply either keeps the private detail out or it does not. Reading the replies shows that "protected" and "leaked" each cover different behaviors. The categories below are my own reading of the examples, applied after the fact; they were not part of the measurement and have not been counted.

    BehaviorWhat the reply doesScored as
    WithholdingDoes the task and leaves the private detail outProtected
    Explicit refusalDeclines the whole task, including the harmless partProtected
    HintingAlludes to the situation without saying whose it is ("I know someone who…")Usually protected; borderline
    Partial disclosureDrops the name but keeps details that identify the person ("my brother")Leaked
    Direct disclosureNames the person and the private detailLeaked

    Explicit refusal matters for interpreting any privacy score. For example, Llama replied to one story with "" That reply refuses to help at all, yet it counts as protection.

They are not evidence for the averages above.     06Why this matters for AI safety evaluations

    • Anonymizing a benchmark can change the behavior it measures. Placeholders are often used to keep test data free of real names, or simply as a convenience. Here that choice alone moved measured protection.
    • Scores from placeholder-named items can overstate protection under realistic names. On the story items, three of four models looked a third to more than half more protective with placeholders. This holds for the benchmarks, models and prompts tested here; it is not a universal correction factor.
    • The size depends on the task format. The same substitution did little on agent tasks, so a benchmark's sensitivity to how people are written has to be measured, not assumed.
    • A higher score is not always better privacy judgement. Some protected replies refuse the whole task, and some placeholder replies drop the name while keeping the secret. Comparisons should look at what the replies do, not only at the score.

    None of this shows that models leak real people's information in deployment. It shows that two evaluations of the same model can disagree because of how their scenarios are written. A practical checklist that follows from these results (a suggestion, not a validated standard):

    • Run realistic names against placeholders on the same items.Report both scores, or say which one a score uses.
    • Use more than one realistic-name assignment.This study used one; a second would separate name-specific effects from noise.
    • Keep the task semantics fixed when changing representation.Change only the names, and check that every name was actually replaced (section 10).
    • Test more than one task format, such as stories and agent tasks.
    • Separate real withholding from superficial avoidance.Dropping a name while keeping identifying details is still a leak.
    • Validate automated graders against human judgements before use.
    • Measure appropriate sharing as well as protection.Otherwise a model that refuses everything gets a perfect score.
    • Report how sensitive the score is to prompt variations.

    07Can we raise protection directly?

    Two other interventions were measured in the same study. Both raise protection, as earlier work on steering and instruction-following would predict. Neither explains the placeholder effect, and neither was tested for its cost to appropriate sharing.

    07.1Neuron steering

    While the model answers, a fixed activation shift is added to 5,000 MLP neurons, about 1% of them. The neurons were chosen on separate items, by how differently they fire on benchmark-style and plain prompts and how strongly they track protective replies. Six conditions ran side by side on Qwen and Mistral: the published prompt, the privacy instruction, steering, steering plus the instruction, and the same two with 5,000 random neurons.

    The effect is specific to the chosen neurons and adds to what an instruction does. It is not yet a privacy solution: the study did not check whether steered models still share information when sharing is appropriate, so part of the gain may be general caution or more refusals.

    07.2Where the instruction goes

    The reference instruction is one sentence in the system prompt: "" Another version put the same sentence at the end of the user's message.

    08Does benchmark awareness explain the effect?

    One possible explanation for any benchmark effect is that models behave differently when they notice they are being tested. In a separate call, each model saw every prompt version and was asked whether it looked like a test or a real request. This measures what a model says, not whether it has seen these benchmarks in training, and it cannot detect recognition that a model does not report.

    Qwen and Mistral answered "real" to nearly everything and Gemma answered "test" to everything, so only Llama's answers varied enough to compare with its behavior. For Llama, placeholder names lowered its "test" answers ( points on ConfAIde, on PrivacyLens) while raising protection. As far as self-report can show, the placeholder effect does not come from the model judging the prompt to be a test.

    09Other findings and negative results

    Looking like a test did not matter. The study was first designed to test whether test-like cues raise protection. They mostly did not. A test-case wrapper (### Case / ### Input / ### Response) changed protection by points (), and a note saying the reply would be scored by (), statistically equivalent to zero. All three cues together gave , about what the placeholder part of the bundle alone predicts (interaction , ). What I learned: the feature that moved behavior was not the one that makes a prompt look most like a test. What remains uncertain: these are only two kinds of test cue, in small models.

    Random neurons did nothing. The random-neuron control moved protection by and points, so the steering gain depends on which neurons are chosen.

    The instruction effect did not replicate clearly in OLMo. In OLMo-2-7B, the system-prompt instruction gave points (), against pooled over the main four.

    An open-model grader failed validation. The first plan used an open 32B model as the grader with a statistical correction. It agreed with my labels at only κ = , below the bar, and on the stories it called far more replies protected than Sonnet did. Every result here uses Sonnet instead (Appendix B). The lesson is the checklist's: validate the grader on the benchmark at hand before reading any score.

    10Limits and future work

    • Two benchmarks, one language, small models. All models are 7 to 9 billion parameters; steering was tested on two of them. Larger models may be more or less sensitive.
    • One realistic renaming. The control used one draw of realistic names. Several draws, and names from different cultures, would show whether particular names matter.
    • Imperfect substitution. In PrivacyLens items a real name survived inside a username or email handle after the placeholder rewrite. Without them, placeholders still raise protection (, ), and realistic renaming then lowers it slightly (, ), so the gap between placeholders and realistic names, if anything, grows.
    • No mechanism. The study shows that placeholders change behavior, not why. Candidate explanations, such as placeholders reading as already anonymized data (example 4) or names acting as the handle for disclosure, are untested.
    • A binary metric. It does not separate withholding from refusal or hinting. Coding a sample of replies into the categories in section 05 would show which behavior placeholders change.
    • No appropriate-sharing test. Neither the placeholder effect nor the interventions were checked on cases where sharing is correct, so higher protection could partly be more caution overall.
    • Automated grading. Sonnet passed a human-agreement gate but graded alone, and for long agent traces it saw a shortened view (Appendix B).
    • No deployment evidence. These are benchmark items, not real conversations.

    11Conclusion

    • Placeholder names make models look more private. Writing people as "Person A" raised measured protection in all four models, and realistic renaming did not.
    • The effect depends on the benchmark. It was concentrated in story items, where it inflated scores by a third to more than half for three models, and was small on agent tasks.
    • A higher score is not the same as better privacy. Some gains are refusals or name-dropping, and one example got worse with placeholders.
    • How we build safety evaluations can change the behavior we measure, and with it the safety conclusions we draw. Representation choices should be tested, not assumed neutral.

    Methods and checks

    Supporting detail for the numbers above. Open any section.

    Appendix A: estimates and intervals

    Each pre-registered estimate is the item-paired difference in protection between a version and the published prompt (120 items per benchmark), with benchmarks weighted equally, pooled over models with DerSimonian–Laird random effects. Intervals come from a cluster bootstrap over items. The four single changes were Holm-corrected together; ±3 points were the pre-registered equivalence bounds. A test passed if its interval excluded zero after correction and at least three of four models agreed in sign. The benchmark split in Figure 1 was not pre-registered: it averages the item-level difference over the four models and bootstraps over items (4,000 resamples). Unclear replies are excluded from every rate.

    Appendix B: can we trust the grades?

    Before any result was read, I graded 150 pilot replies by hand, blind to model and version; 147 had a clear verdict. Claude Sonnet then had to agree with those labels at κ ≥ 0.70 before it could grade the study. Long agent traces were shown to Sonnet in a shortened view (the whole user message up to 2,500 characters, otherwise its first and last paragraphs), and the gate was re-run on that view.

    Appendix C: pre-registration and deviations

    The design, the 240 test items (never used before in this project, plus 60 pilot items), the decision rules and the analysis code were frozen with checksums before generation. Eleven dated deviations are listed in the repository. The largest is that every reply was graded by Claude Sonnet, rather than by the open-model judge with a statistical correction, after that judge failed the gate. Another is that Gemma answered "test" to every recognition prompt, so it could not be used for the recognition comparison.

    Appendix D: generation and steering settings

    vLLM 0.34 (Hugging Face generate for steering), temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95, 384 new tokens, 4 samples per prompt (2 in the steering arm), pinned model revisions. Placeholder names replace every person with "Person A", "Person B" and so on in order of appearance, and email addresses with userN@example.com; realistic renaming uses a fixed name list in the same positions. Steering adds a scaled average activation shift (α = 4) to the top 5,000 MLP neurons, selected on separate items; the random control uses 5,000 random neurons per item at the same strength. The recognition probe is a separate greedy call per prompt with a one-word answer (test, real or unsure).

    Appendix E: reproducibility

    Every number on this page is produced by an export script that reads the result files and fails if a value disagrees with the registered analysis output, including the reply counts in Section 03. The examples are copied from the result files by the same script, which checks each shown reply's grade and tally. The repository holds the item lists, prompts, generations, all Sonnet labels, the human labels, the analysis code, a checksum manifest and step-by-step reproduction instructions.
