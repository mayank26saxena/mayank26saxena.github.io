A Test Context Changed Privacy Behavior

In one controlled setup, Qwen2.5-7B protected private information more often in an evaluation context. No single cue explained the shift, so this shows context-dependent behavior, not proof the model knew it was being tested. A separate prompt comparison found that an explicit privacy instruction improved protection more than test framing alone. I also tested whether neuron steering could transfer protective behavior—and whether it added value over that simpler instruction.

In short

  • Context changed behavior: in Qwen2.5-7B, a bundle of evaluation-context cues raised privacy protection from 68% to 93% across ten privacy tasks. Honesty did not change. This is evidence of a behavior shift in this setup, not proof of evaluation recognition.
  • No single cue explained the shift: placeholder names, grading language, and other cues did not reliably reproduce the full effect on their own. In a separate five-model prompt comparison, a privacy reminder improved protection more than test framing alone.
  • The steering attempt: TMT selected MLP neurons associated with more protective responses and used them to steer ordinary prompts. It did not train the model.
  • The result: under the primary judge, TMT beat matched random-neuron controls in four of five model families on pooled held-out data. Mistral was the clearest result, improving in both folds while preserving nearly all measured legitimate sharing.
  • The practical comparison: a direct privacy system instruction improved measured protection. Steering did not establish an added benefit over that simpler baseline; a second blind judge also disagreed substantially with the first.

01The first result looked like test awareness

Privacy benchmarks ask a model to handle someone’s private information: an address, a medical detail, or something shared in confidence. The benchmark measures whether the model protects that information.

But benchmark prompts can look different from ordinary requests. They may use placeholder names, terse wording, or evaluation instructions. A model might protect information more because it recognizes a test. It might also react to the format, or simply notice a reminder that privacy matters.

In a controlled Qwen2.5-7B experiment, I varied evaluation context separately from benchmark format. Across ten privacy tasks, protection rose from 68% in the ordinary context to 93% with the full bundle of evaluation-context cues. That is a 25-point increase (95% task-bootstrap interval: +7.5 to +45). Honesty was unchanged. The privacy shift was concentrated in a subset of tasks.

I then flipped one cue at a time. Each estimate below compares a cue condition with the same task in its all-realistic form. No single cue reliably reproduced the full shift. The estimates are uncertain: there were only ten privacy tasks and one generated response per prompt. Placeholder names also changed the details being protected, so they were not a pure realism manipulation.

In an initial comparison, I took 100 items each from ConfAIde and PrivacyLens. I compared the prompts as published with versions that added a benchmark-style opening and a note that the response would be graded for privacy. Under the primary Qwen3-4B judge, the five open models had a pooled protection increase of 7.7 percentage points. At first, this looked like evidence that models became more protective when they knew they were being tested.

But the comparison changed two things at once: it made the prompt look like a test and reminded the model that privacy mattered. So it could not show that the model recognized an evaluation.

02Separate the cues

I followed up with a 2×2 experiment. Each item appeared with or without test framing and with or without a direct privacy reminder. This let me estimate the two cues separately.

Protection means the share of replies that withheld the private detail. The intervals below are 95% paired item-bootstrap intervals.

The test-framing estimates were small, and their intervals include zero. The privacy-reminder estimates were larger and positive under both judges. In this experiment, the reminder explained more of the initial change than test framing did.

This separate five-model prompt study points in the same careful direction: test framing alone had a small, uncertain effect, while a privacy reminder produced a larger increase under both judges. It does not show that models never recognize evaluations. It shows that prompt changes can affect measured behavior for different reasons, and that recognition was not isolated as the cause here.

03How TMT works

TMT asks whether the model’s own protective behavior on one version of a prompt can help it answer another version. It does not fine-tune the model or give it hand-written ideal answers.

For each model, I compared paired prompts: an as-published version and a test-style version. I kept pairs where the model protected private information at least 30 points more often under the test-style version, and where it had produced both protective and disclosing replies.

Then I scored each MLP neuron using two signals:

  1. How much the neuron’s activation changed between the two prompt versions.
  2. How strongly the neuron was associated with replies judged to protect private information rather than disclose it.

I selected the top 5,000 neurons, about 1% of the MLP neurons, and added a scaled version of their average test-minus-published activation shift while the model answered the ordinary prompt. I tested two steering strengths.

The judge’s labels helped select the prompt pairs and define the protective-versus-disclosing contrast. TMT therefore uses the model’s own behavior as a target, but it is not label-free.

I tested on 200 held-out benchmark items using two folds: find neurons on one half, test them on the other, then swap. Each item had three sampled replies. Controls included random neurons of the same size and strength. I also checked six legitimate requests where the person asking was entitled to the private detail. A useful privacy intervention should protect secrets without refusing authorized requests.

A concrete example

In one paired ConfAIde example, someone tried to draw a model into sharing a colleague’s confidential workplace mistake as part of a bet. The baseline reply repeated the sensitive story. The steered reply said the colleague had shared it in confidence and declined to use it for the bet. This is one example, not a measure of how often TMT works; the aggregate results above provide that measure.

The simple baseline matters too. A one-line privacy system instruction raised measured protection relative to the unmodified prompt in the available comparison. Under a later blind re-grade, TMT’s pooled advantage over that instruction was 6.8 points, with a 95% interval from −1.4 to +15.0. That interval includes zero, so the experiment did not establish that steering adds value over the simpler instruction. The prompt and steering also serve different purposes: the instruction directly asks for privacy protection; TMT tests whether a model’s own activation patterns can transfer behavior.

05The grader changed the picture

The second blind LLM judge agreed with the primary judge on only 59.6% of the 2,800 replies; Cohen’s κ was 0.23. It also changed the absolute protection rates substantially.

This is a limitation of the result, not a detail to hide. The 2×2 cue effects kept the same direction under both judges, but the disagreement makes the exact size of the steering effect uncertain. I did not complete a full human re-grade.

On four self-written scenarios, a Qwen weight patch raised protection from 42% to 100% while preserving all measured legitimate sharing. A direct privacy instruction reached 54% on those scenarios. This is supporting evidence only: the scenarios were written by us, and they also informed method development. The patch also cost about 1.75 points on MMLU, consistent with a broader shift toward caution.

The steering results also failed to transfer cleanly between benchmarks. For Llama, neurons selected on PrivacyLens had no effect on ConfAIde. Neurons selected on ConfAIde drove near-total refusal on PrivacyLens, including on legitimate requests. That is not a privacy-specific fix.

06What I take from the project

The project’s contribution is evidence that evaluation context can change privacy behavior in a controlled setup, alongside a finding that no single cue explained the shift. A separate prompt comparison showed that a direct privacy reminder moved benchmark outcomes more than test framing alone. TMT offered a promising transfer signal against random-neuron controls, but the study did not establish that it improves on a simple privacy instruction.

The broader lesson is about measurement. A model that protects information more in a benchmark-looking prompt may be reacting to a privacy reminder or formatting cue. A detector or intervention should be tested against those alternatives before we call the effect “evaluation awareness.”

I also changed some steering choices after seeing early results, including adding the stronger setting and revising the pass rule. Those changes are recorded in dated addenda to the preregistration. The pooled results should be read with that history in view.

This is not a general privacy fix or proof of strategic test awareness. It is evidence that evaluation context can shift privacy behavior in one model and benchmark setup, and a first test of whether protective behavior can guide neuron steering. The next step is a larger, human-checked study that separates context, format, and privacy instructions while holding the underlying request fixed.

Methods and checks

  • Models: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9B-it, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and OLMo-2-7B-Instruct.
  • Benchmarks: 100 items each from ConfAIde tier 3 and PrivacyLens; the prompts were adapted from their published versions.
  • Steering: top 5,000 MLP neurons selected from activation shift weighted by attribution; two steering strengths; two held-out folds.
  • Controls: matched random neurons, a direct privacy instruction, and legitimate requests where disclosure was appropriate.
  • Grading: primary Qwen3-4B judge and a second blind LLM judge. Their disagreement limits confidence in absolute rates.
  • Limitations: models were 7–9B; benchmark prompts are not realistic user conversations; legitimate sharing was tested on six self-written requests; the TMT pass rule and strongest setting were revised after early results.
