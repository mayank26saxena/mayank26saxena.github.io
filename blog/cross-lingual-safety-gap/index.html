AI Chatbots Refuse Harmful Requests Less Often in Hindi

We gave the same harmful requests in English and Hindi to two open chat models: Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Both refused less often in Hindi. We studied what they did instead, what happened inside the models, and whether steering could increase refusals.

Chat models are mainly trained to refuse harmful requests in English. Studies find that refusals weaken in other languages (Yong et al. 2023; Deng et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024), including Indic ones (IndicSafe; IndicSafeEval; SurakshaEval). We ask what models do instead of refusing, and what changes inside them. This study adds three things: a side-by-side comparison of how refusals fail in two models, a look at their internal signals, and a steering intervention that raises Hindi refusals but also lifts English ones.

01How we measured it

We studied Hindi with two open 8B models, pre-registered each hypothesis test, and report the failures too. The results cover these two models, this request set and greedy decoding (always picking the most likely next token, a word or part of a word), not all chat models. “Refusal” means our judge labelled a reply as an explicit refusal; it does not measure harm to users.

Each harmful request was asked in Hindi and in English. Human annotators wrote most of them in Hindi (Aya Red-teaming; Aakanksha et al. 2024) with a human English translation, so we compare the languages on the same request. We screened every item for being a real request, a faithful translation, and one the model understood: items it misunderstood show the same gap. The held-out set is 404 harmful and 169 harmless request pairs, identical for both models.

Each reply gets one of four labels, checked in this order, so a reply that answers the request counts as complied even if it adds a warning.

An AI judge assigned the labels, and a fluent Hindi speaker checked a sample, because AI judges can be less reliable across languages (Fu & Liu 2025). Agreement was good for explicit refusals (κ = 0.79, even blind) and weaker for the line between a soft refusal and a reply that warns but still answers (Appendix B). 03What replaces the refusal?

Explicit refusal drops sharply in both models. Where does it go?

In Hindi, Qwen often warns, lectures or changes the subject instead of refusing clearly. Llama complies with harmful Hindi requests 1.9 times as often as Qwen. The split between soft refusal and compliance rests on a hard label boundary, so read it as indicative (Appendix B).

04Where do Hindi requests land inside the model?

Chat models carry a refusal direction in their internal activity, linked to whether they refuse (Arditi et al. 2024). We fitted it on English requests, used the layer where removing it cuts refusal most (layer 14 in Qwen, layer 10 in Llama), and asked where Hindi requests land. Each prompt gets a score on the direction: higher means the model’s internal activity points more toward refusing.

In both models the direction still ranks harmful Hindi requests above harmless ones (Figure 2), but the average score gap between them is only about a third as large as in English ( for Qwen, for Llama). These readings are hypotheses: they rest on two models and one kind of probe, and the score does not show that a model recognises harm (Zhao et al. 2025). Earlier work also finds shared refusal directions that are weaker in lower-resource languages (Wang et al. 2025; Aziz et al. 2026).

05Can a targeted push make the models refuse more in Hindi?

We turned these findings into two repairs. The aim: more explicit refusal of harmful Hindi requests, without refusing harmless ones or breaking replies.

05.1Gated steering

The repair is gated steering (Turner et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2025). A gate reads each prompt’s refusal signal; if the prompt looks harmful, a fixed direction is added to the model’s activity while it writes the reply. We chose settings on development requests by rules set in advance (Appendix D), then tested each once on the held-out requests. For Qwen the push is toward explicit refusal: we fitted a direction on English replies that separates explicit from soft refusals, and add it to the model’s activity. For Llama it strengthens the refusal direction, with the gate threshold set on Hindi prompts: an English threshold opened on only 1% of Llama’s harmful Hindi requests.

05.2What the repairs achieve

Both repairs increased explicit refusals in Hindi on held-out requests (Figure 4). We also track English, because a push that made the model refuse more of everything would raise Hindi refusal without fixing anything specific to Hindi. English rose too, so Hindi caught up by much less than it gained. The repairs did cut compliance with harmful Hindi requests, from to for Llama and from to for Qwen.

Figure 5 shows why English rises too: the gate asks whether a prompt looks harmful, and English harmful prompts score at least as high as Hindi ones. Llama’s Hindi-set threshold lets through about half of harmful Hindi requests by design, and none of the 169 harmless ones.

Side effects. We ran three checks.

  • General ability. On 300 Global-MMLU questions per language, accuracy did not change (Qwen in Hindi and in English; Llama in Hindi and in English).
  • Harmless requests. Refusals stayed within our 3-point limit.
  • A weak test. The gate never opened on Qwen’s MMLU questions, so those replies could not change. Forcing steering on every question moved Qwen’s accuracy from 51.3% to 52.3% in Hindi and from 64.0% to 63.7% in English, within noise.

06Limits, and what’s next

  • Scope. Two 8B models, one request set and greedy decoding, with one AI judge and one human grader. “Refusal” is a judged reply category, not harm, user outcomes or deployed safety.
  • Steering. It changed replies here; we have not shown it improves multilingual safety more broadly. The harmless-request and ability checks are weak.
  • Languages. Hindi is the only language studied in depth (Spanish control, Qwen only: Appendix C). The per-layer Llama curves come from an exploratory run added after the registered studies.
  • What didn’t hold up (Appendix E). A sealed Bengali test failed its registered check, and a small set of neurons behind the English–Hindi difference moved English refusals, not Hindi ones.
  • Next. Compare with simpler fixes such as a safety prompt or translating to English, which can rival steering (Wu et al. 2025), test on harmless requests that sound harmful (Balani & Panda 2026), build a gate selective for Hindi, and run a fresh confirmation set for Llama.

How sure are we? These are best estimates from 404 requests, so they could shift if we had asked different requests. The margin of error on each difference is at most about ± points, so a smaller difference could be noise. Qwen’s catch-up in Hindi ( points) is within that margin and may be zero; Llama’s ( points) is not. We chose the repair settings on separate development requests and tested them once; held-out gains were about 80% of the best development gains.

Appendix B: can we trust the labels?

Many results depend on separating a soft refusal from a reply that warns but then answers. This is difficult. A fluent Hindi reader blindly graded 60 Llama replies. Initial agreement was κ = 0.64, below our registered threshold of 0.7. Most disagreements involved warnings followed by answers. The grader then reapplied the rubric: a warning followed by an answer counts as compliance. Agreement rose to 0.89. We report both results.

The headline results use explicit refusals, which had stronger agreement in the blind check (κ = 0.79). Qwen’s judge passed its validation check with κ = 0.82.

Appendix D: how hard to push

We tried several steering strengths on development requests and kept the strongest one that stayed inside limits set in advance (broken replies within +5 points, harmless refusal within +3).

Appendix E: the failed checks in detail
  • Bengali test. We checked Qwen’s repair on a sealed Bengali test set. It raised explicit refusal by 23.6 points but also made 8.1 points more replies garbled, over our 5-point limit, so the registered verdict is a fail. A follow-up on new Bengali requests did not repeat the damage, which softens the result but does not erase it.
  • “Hindi switch.” We looked for a small set of neurons behind the English–Hindi difference. 100 of 307,200 neurons carried three quarters of it. But changing them raised Qwen’s English refusals by 15.2 points and its Hindi refusals by only 1.2, so they are not a Hindi-specific lever.
Appendix F: how we tried to keep ourselves honest
  • Rules first. Before each experiment we recorded what we would measure, how we would choose a setting and what would count as success; later changes went into dated addenda. The per-layer Llama curves in Figure 2 were added afterwards. They are descriptive and test no hypothesis.
  • No harmful text. Requests are summarised, never quoted. The only reply text shown is the redacted excerpts in sections 02 and 05, with anything usable for harm blacked out.
